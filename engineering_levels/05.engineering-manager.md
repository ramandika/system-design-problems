# 05 — Engineering Manager

**Essence**: owns outcomes through people, systems, and priorities. The unit of leverage is
a team, not a diff or a design doc.

## Scope

- A team (5–10 engineers) and its outcomes: shipped work, system health, growth of its
  members, and its place in the org.
- Accountable for: what the team works on, whether it's achievable, whether people grow,
  and whether the org can trust the team's systems.
- NOT accountable for being the best engineer on the team — the design bar belongs to the
  [Senior](03.senior-engineer.md)/[Principal](04.principal-engineer.md) track.

## System design competency

- **Can evaluate, not necessarily author.** Reads a design doc and asks the questions that
  matter: what breaks first, what does this cost to run, what did we NOT consider, is the
  scope the smallest that solves the problem? (Same checklist as the study cases' deep dives.)
- Understands the system well enough to make priority calls: what earns the right to be
  on-call pain, what is safe to defer, when a quality problem blocks a business goal.
- Can spot when a team is taking a design shortcut that will become a multi-quarter debt —
  even if they couldn't have designed the correct version themselves.
- Does not need to win technical debates; needs to make sure technical debates reach a
  decision fast, by the right people, with the trade-off written down.

## Technical execution

- Runs the delivery mechanics: scoping, sequencing, dependencies, and saying "this roadmap
  doesn't fit the team we have" before it fails, not after.
- Owns the team's engineering health: on-call load, technical debt budget (a real allocation,
  not a vibe), incident follow-through (postmortems → actual design changes).
- Keeps the loop closed between incidents and level definitions: recurring outages are a
  competency gap — at whatever level it belongs.

## Judgment signals

- Coaching you can cite: members grow into bigger scope with specific moves behind it —
  scope-giving, honest assessment against the ladder (this repo's files), a growth plan
  someone can name. Promotions follow from that; they are an outcome, not the graded signal.
- Says the uncomfortable calibration out loud: "X is performing at Mid, not Senior" — early,
  privately, with a growth plan.
- Distinguishes reversible from irreversible people decisions (same skill as Senior's
  reversible/irreversible technical calls — now applied to humans).
- Removes blockers they don't understand fully; delegates the how, owns the what and why.
- When the system breaks, they get the right *people* moving, not themselves at the keyboard
  (unless the team is 2 people, in which case they're at the keyboard too).

## Not expected

- Writing most of the code, or being the design authority by default. If an EM is the only
  one who can set the team's technical direction, there's a hiring gap, not a management win.
- Micromanaging designs that their Seniors own.
- Confusing "my team is busy" with "my team wins" — outcomes, not activity, are the metric.

## Growth to next level

Growth is a bigger unit of leverage: from one team to a group of teams — building other
EMs, setting the org's management bar. Same craft, larger blast radius; whether that's a
promotion or a role change depends on the org.

The interface with the IC levels:

- The EM + Senior pair is the core of a healthy team: EM owns *what and who*; Senior owns
  *how the system is built*; each is genuinely confused without the other.
- A Principal hands the EM hard truths about scope and debt; the EM hands the Principal
  real costs about people and roadmap. Neither wins the argument by rank — the trade-off
  must be written down to decide.
- The same design discipline applies to both: smallest thing that works, name what was
  skipped, and state the trade-off explicitly.
